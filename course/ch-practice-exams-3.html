    Exercises Questions

    1.

    Determine whether the vector
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is a linear combination of
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    If it is, find scalars that express \(\mathbf{b}\) as such a combination.
    Hint.
    Try to solve the vector equation \(a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{b}\) by comparing components.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes, \(\mathbf{b}\) is a linear combination of \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We solve
    \begin{equation*} a\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\\2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This gives the system
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} a + 2b \amp = 3 \\ b \amp = 1 \\ a \amp = 2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The equations are consistent, so \(\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{v}_1 + 1\mathbf{v}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the augmented matrix
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Write a system of linear equations represented by this matrix.
      🔗
    2. Identify which variables are pivot variables and which are free.
      🔗
    3. Explain why performing row operations on this matrix does not change the solution set of the system.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Each row corresponds to one equation, and pivot variables correspond to leading entries.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The system has two pivot variables and one free variable, and row operations preserve the solution set.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The matrix represents the system
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x + 2y - z \amp = 3 \\ y + 2z \amp = 1. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The leading entries occur in the \(x\) and \(y\) columns, so \(x\) and \(y\) are pivot variables, while \(z\) is free.
    🔗
    Row operations correspond to rewriting equations using algebraic steps such as swapping equations, scaling them, or replacing one equation with a combination of others. These operations do not change the set of solutions, only the way the system is written.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Consider the matrix equation
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Rewrite this equation as a vector equation involving the columns of the matrix.
      🔗
    2. Determine whether the equation has a solution.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Think of the right-hand side as a linear combination of the columns of the matrix. Solve for the coefficients.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes, the equation has a solution; the right-hand side is a linear combination of the columns.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Writing the equation in vector form gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1 \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\1\\5\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Solving the system yields \(x_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(x_2 = 1\text{,}\) and \(x_3 = 1\text{.}\) Therefore, the vector on the right lies in the span of the columns, and the matrix equation is consistent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 4 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Decide whether the equation \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has a solution by interpreting it as a vector equation.
    Hint.
    Ask whether \(\mathbf{b}\) can be written as a linear combination of the columns of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes. The equation does have a solution, \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The corresponding vector equation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1 \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\7\\2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Solving this system leads to a solution \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{,}\) showing that \(\mathbf{b}\) is in the span of the columns of \(A\text{.}\) Therefore, the matrix equation has a solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Describe the set \(\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}\) geometrically.
      🔗
    2. Determine whether the vector
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      lies in this span.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    The span consists of all linear combinations of the given vectors. To test membership, try to solve \(a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{w}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The span is a plane through the origin, and \(\mathbf{w}\) lies in this plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) are not scalar multiples, their span is a plane through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To see whether \(\mathbf{w}\) lies in the span, we solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + b \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This leads to the system
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} a + 2b \amp = 3 \\ b \amp = 1 \\ a + 3b \amp = 4. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The system is consistent with \(a = 1\) and \(b = 1\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\mathbf{w}\) lies in the span.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Determine whether the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are linearly independent. Justify your answer using the definition of linear independence.
    Hint.
    Ask whether a nontrivial linear combination of the vectors can equal the zero vector. Look for obvious scalar multiples.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vectors are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vector \(\mathbf{v}_2\) is twice \(\mathbf{v}_1\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\mathbf{v}_2 - 2\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{0}\) is a nontrivial linear combination that equals the zero vector.
    🔗
    Since not all coefficients are zero, the set of vectors is linearly dependent by definition.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Suppose three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) all lie in the same plane through the origin.
    1. Can these three vectors be linearly independent? Explain.
      🔗
    2. Explain how your answer follows from the definition of linear independence.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Think about how many directions are needed to describe all vectors in a plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    No, the three vectors cannot be linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A plane through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) can be described as the span of two linearly independent vectors. Any third vector in the same plane can be written as a linear combination of those two.
    🔗
    Therefore, one of the three vectors can be expressed as a linear combination of the others, which means the set is linearly dependent by definition.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    Determine whether the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are linearly independent. You must justify your answer using reduced row echelon form.
    Hint.
    Form a matrix whose columns are the given vectors and compute its reduced row echelon form.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vectors are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Form the matrix with the vectors as columns:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Row‑reducing this matrix yields the reduced row echelon form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Since there is a free variable, and the system is consistent, there are infinitely many solutions. So, \(c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}\) when \(c_1=1\text{,}\) \(c_2=-1\text{,}\) and \(c_3=1\) (for example). Therefore, the vectors are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    For what value(s) of \(k\) are the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ k \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ k \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    linearly independent?
    Hint.
    Form a matrix using the vectors as columns and determine when a pivot fails to appear.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vectors are linearly independent for all values of \(k\) except \(k = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Place the vectors as columns of a matrix:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ k \amp 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp k \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Row‑reducing yields a pivot in every column unless \(k - 2 = 0\text{.}\) When \(k = 2\text{,}\) one column becomes a linear combination of the others.
    🔗
    Therefore, the vectors are linearly independent for all \(k \neq 2\text{,}\) and linearly dependent when \(k = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Let \(T : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) be the linear transformation defined by
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}. \end{equation*}
    1. Compute \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2)\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Explain how these images determine the transformation \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Multiply the matrix by each standard basis vector. Recall that every vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be written as a linear combination of \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) These vectors determine the entire transformation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \quad T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Every vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^2\) can be written as \(\mathbf{x} = a\mathbf{e}_1 + b\mathbf{e}_2\text{.}\) Linearity implies \(T(\mathbf{x}) = aT(\mathbf{e}_1) + bT(\mathbf{e}_2)\text{.}\) Therefore, knowing the images of the standard basis vectors completely determines the transformation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    11.

    Let \(T : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) be the linear transformation defined by
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}. \end{equation*}
    1. Compute \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2)\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Describe the geometric effect of \(T\) on vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Describe the image of the unit square under \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute the images of the standard basis vectors. The image of the unit square is determined by the images of these vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) The transformation is a shear, and the unit square maps to a parallelogram.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The transformation leaves horizontal vectors unchanged while shifting vertical vectors to the right, producing a shear. Since the unit square is spanned by \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\text{,}\) its image is the parallelogram spanned by \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    12.

    The figure below shows the image of the unit square in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) under a linear transformation \(T\text{.}\)
    1. Write the matrix of \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Explain how the diagram determines this matrix.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    The columns of the matrix of a linear transformation are the images of the standard basis vectors \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The matrix of \(T\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    From the diagram, we see that \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For a linear transformation, the matrix is formed by placing \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2)\) as the first and second columns, respectively. Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    is the matrix of \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    13.

    Let \(T : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) be the linear transformation that projects every vector onto the line \(y = x\text{.}\)
    1. Any vector on the line \(y=x\) can be written in the form \(t\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Use this to explain why \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) must have this form.
      🔗
    2. Find the value of \(t\) such that the vector \(\mathbf{e}_1 - t\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) is perpendicular to the line \(y=x\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Use your result to compute \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) and \(T(\mathbf{e}_2)\text{,}\) then write the matrix of \(T\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    A vector is perpendicular to the line \(y=x\) if it is perpendicular to \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Use the dot product.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and the matrix of \(T\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} \tfrac12 \amp \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 \amp \tfrac12 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the image of any vector under the projection lies on the line \(y=x\text{,}\) we know that \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = t\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) for some scalar \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vector from \(T(\mathbf{e}_1)\) to \(\mathbf{e}_1\) must be perpendicular to the line \(y=x\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left( \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} - t\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} \right) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = 0. \end{equation*}
    Computing the dot product gives \(1 - 2t = 0\text{,}\) so \(t = \tfrac12\text{.}\) Thus, \(T(\mathbf{e}_1) = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By symmetry, \(\mathbf{e}_2\) projects to the same point on the line, so \(T(\mathbf{e}_2) = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Placing these vectors as columns gives the matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac12 \amp \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 \amp \tfrac12 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}

    • Yes! This is the definition of span.

    • Correct.

    • The only way this statement could be true for all possible scalars is if all the vectors involved are zero. Otherwise, changing a scalar is going to change the resulting linear combination.
