    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Verify that \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) is an eigenvector of \(A\text{,}\) and find its corresponding eigenvalue.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute \(A\mathbf{v}\) and check whether it is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute \(A\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Since \(A\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{v},\) the vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector and the corresponding eigenvalue is \(\lambda=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    Find the eigenvalues of the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Eigenvalues satisfy \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(\lambda=2\) and \(\lambda=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute \(\det(A-\lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 4-\lambda \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (4-\lambda)(1-\lambda)+2.\)
    🔗
    Expanding, \((4-\lambda)(1-\lambda)+2 = \lambda^2 -5\lambda +6.\)
    🔗
    Setting this equal to zero, \(\lambda^2 -5\lambda +6 = 0,\) which factors as \((\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)=0.\)
    🔗
    Therefore, the eigenvalues are \(\lambda=2\) and \(\lambda=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Describe geometrically how \(A\) acts on vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Identify all eigenvalues of \(A\) and describe the corresponding eigenvectors geometrically.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    Examine what happens to vectors lying on each coordinate axis, and interpret the sign and magnitude of the resulting scalar multiples.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The matrix stretches vectors on the \(x\)-axis by a factor of 2 and reflects vectors on the \(y\)-axis across the origin. The eigenvalues are \(2\) and \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The matrix \(A\) acts independently on each coordinate. A vector \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\) is sent to \(\begin{bmatrix}2x\\-y\end{bmatrix}.\) Geometrically, this stretches space horizontally by a factor of 2 and reflects vectors vertically across the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For vectors on the \(x\)-axis, we have \(A\begin{bmatrix}x\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2x\\0\end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix}x\\0\end{bmatrix}.\) Every nonzero vector on the \(x\)-axis is therefore an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      For vectors on the \(y\)-axis, \(A\begin{bmatrix}0\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\-y\end{bmatrix} = -1\begin{bmatrix}0\\y\end{bmatrix}.\) Every nonzero vector on the \(y\)-axis is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(-1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Thus, \(A\) has two distinct eigenvalues with eigendirections aligned with the coordinate axes, one producing stretching and the other producing a reflection.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    4.
    Consider the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix},\) and the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\0\end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    1. Show that \(\mathbf{v}_1\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{v}_2\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_3\) are eigenvectors of \(A\text{,}\) and find their associated eigenvalues.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Express the vector \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\\-4\\2 \end{bmatrix}\) as a linear combination of the eigenvectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use this expression to compute \(A\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) \(A^2\mathbf{x}\text{,}\) and \(A^{-1}\mathbf{x}\) as linear combinations of the eigenvectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    For part (a), check whether \(A\mathbf{v}_i\) is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{v}_i\text{.}\) For parts (b) and (c), work entirely in the eigenvector basis.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(4\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) and \(1\text{.}\) The vector \(\mathbf{x}\) can be written as \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}_1 + 2\mathbf{v}_2 + 2\mathbf{v}_3.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We compute:
      \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} = 4\mathbf{v}_1, \end{equation*}
      so \(\mathbf{v}_1\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Next,
      \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\-4\\0\end{bmatrix} = 2\mathbf{v}_2, \end{equation*}
      so \(\mathbf{v}_2\) has eigenvalue \(2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Finally,
      \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{v}_3, \end{equation*}
      so the eigenvalue is \(1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We seek scalars \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) such that
      \begin{equation*} a\mathbf{v}_1 + b\mathbf{v}_2 + c\mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix}3\\-4\\2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      This gives
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a + b \\ -2b \\ c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\-4\\2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      Solving, we find \(b=2\text{,}\) \(a=1\text{,}\) and \(c=2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Since each eigenvector satisfies \(A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i\text{,}\) we can apply powers and inverses of \(A\) by scaling each eigenvector. Using \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v}_1 + 2\mathbf{v}_2 + 2\mathbf{v}_3,\) we compute:
      🔗
      \begin{align*} A\mathbf{x} \amp = 1(4\mathbf{v}_1) + 2(2\mathbf{v}_2) + 2(1\mathbf{v}_3) \\ \amp = 4\mathbf{v}_1 + 4\mathbf{v}_2 + 2\mathbf{v}_3. \end{align*}
      🔗
      Similarly,
      \begin{align*} A^2\mathbf{x} \amp = 1(4^2\mathbf{v}_1) + 2(2^2\mathbf{v}_2) + 2(1^2\mathbf{v}_3) \\ \amp = 16\mathbf{v}_1 + 8\mathbf{v}_2 + 2\mathbf{v}_3. \end{align*}
      🔗
      Since \(A^{-1}\mathbf{v}_i = \tfrac{1}{\lambda_i}\mathbf{v}_i\text{,}\) we obtain
      🔗
      \begin{align*} A^{-1}\mathbf{x} \amp = 1\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{v}_1\right) + 2\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}_2\right) + 2(1\mathbf{v}_3) \\ \amp = \tfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 + 2\mathbf{v}_3. \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗

    5.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Find a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue \(\lambda=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Solve \((A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    A basis is \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We form \(A-2I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    The equation \((A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) becomes \(2x_1+x_2=0\text{.}\) Solving gives \(x_2=-2x_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus eigenvectors are multiples of \(\begin{bmatrix}-1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) which forms a basis for the eigenspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    6.
    Compute the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Consider how the determinant of \(A-\lambda I\) simplifies.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(1\text{,}\) \(3\text{,}\) and \(5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(A-\lambda I\) is upper triangular, its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries: \((1-\lambda)(3-\lambda)(5-\lambda).\)
    🔗
    Setting this equal to zero gives the eigenvalues \(\lambda=1\text{,}\) \(\lambda=3\text{,}\) and \(\lambda=5\text{.}\)

    7.
    Suppose a \(2\times2\) matrix \(A\) has a single eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of multiplicity two.
    🔗
    Explain what this means for the action of \(A\) on vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Consider how many independent directions are preserved by the transformation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    All eigenvectors lie along a single line through the origin.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A repeated eigenvalue means that the characteristic polynomial has only one root.
    🔗
    The eigenspace may still be one‑dimensional, meaning there is only one line of vectors that are stretched by the transformation. Vectors not on this line are generally rotated or sheared.
    🔗
    This situation contrasts with matrices that have two distinct eigenvalues, which preserve two independent directions.
    🔗
    For example, consider the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This matrix has a single eigenvalue of multiplicity two. We analyze its eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and geometric behavior.
    🔗
    We compute the characteristic polynomial: \(\det(A-\lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 3-\lambda \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 3-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (3-\lambda)^2.\) Thus, \(\lambda=3\) is the only eigenvalue, with multiplicity two.
    🔗
    To find the eigenvectors, we solve \((A-3I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\text{:}\) \(A-3I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This gives the equation \(x_2=0\text{,}\) so eigenvectors have the form \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\0\end{bmatrix}, \; x\neq 0.\) The eigenspace is therefore one‑dimensional, consisting of all nonzero vectors along the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    Geometrically, the transformation scales vectors by a factor of 3, but also shears vectors that are not on the eigenline. Only vectors lying on the \(x\)-axis preserve their direction.
    🔗
    This example illustrates that a repeated eigenvalue does not guarantee two independent eigenvectors. Even though the eigenvalue has multiplicity two, there is only one invariant direction in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    8.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Determine whether \(A\) is diagonalizable. Justify your answer.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Find the eigenvalues and the dimension of each eigenspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The matrix \(A\) is not diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The characteristic polynomial of \(A\) is \(\det(A-\lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2.\) Thus the only eigenvalue is \(\lambda=2\text{,}\) with algebraic multiplicity 2.
    🔗
    To find the eigenspace, we solve \((A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\text{:}\) \(A-2I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This gives \(x_2=0\text{,}\) so the eigenspace is one‑dimensional. Because there are fewer eigenvectors than the size of the matrix, \(A\) is not diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    9.
    Consider the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Find the eigenvalues of \(A\) and their algebraic multiplicities.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find a basis for each eigenspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Diagonalize \(A\) by finding matrices \(P\) and \(D\) such that \(A = PDP^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    First compute \(\det(A-\lambda I)\text{.}\) Then solve \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) for each eigenvalue. Remember that \(A\) is diagonalizable if it has three linearly independent eigenvectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(3\) with multiplicity 2 and \(1\) with multiplicity 1. The matrix is diagonalizable.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We compute
      \begin{align*} \det(A-\lambda I) \amp = \det \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 2-\lambda \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 3-\lambda \end{bmatrix} \\ \amp = (3-\lambda)\big((2-\lambda)^2-1\big). \end{align*}
      This factors as \((3-\lambda)(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)\text{.}\) Thus the eigenvalues are \(\lambda=3\) (multiplicity 2) and \(\lambda=1\) (multiplicity 1).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For \(\lambda=3\text{,}\) we solve \((A-3I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\text{,}\) which gives \(x_1=x_2\) with \(x_3\) free. A basis for this eigenspace is
      \begin{equation*} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      For \(\lambda=1\text{,}\) solving \((A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) yields \(x_1=-x_2\) and \(x_3=0\text{.}\) A basis for this eigenspace is
      \begin{equation*} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Since there are three linearly independent eigenvectors, \(A\) is diagonalizable. Let
      \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      Then \(A = PDP^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    10.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Diagonalize \(A\) by finding matrices \(P\) and \(D\) such that \(A = PDP^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use the diagonalization to find a formula for \(A^n\text{,}\) where \(n\) is a positive integer.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    The eigenvalues satisfy \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\text{.}\) If \(A=PDP^{-1}\text{,}\) then \(A^n = PD^nP^{-1}\text{.}\) Powers of a diagonal matrix are easy to compute.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(5\) and \(1\text{.}\) A formula for \(A^n\) is
    \begin{equation*} A^n = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 5^n + 1^n \amp 5^n - 1^n \\ 5^n - 1^n \amp 5^n + 1^n \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We compute the characteristic polynomial:
      \begin{align*} \det(A-\lambda I) \amp = \det \begin{bmatrix} 3-\lambda \amp 2 \\ 2 \amp 3-\lambda \end{bmatrix} \\ \amp = (3-\lambda)^2 - 4 \\ \amp = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 5. \end{align*}
      Factoring gives \((\lambda-5)(\lambda-1)=0\text{.}\) Thus the eigenvalues are \(\lambda=5\) and \(\lambda=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      For \(\lambda=5\text{,}\) solving \((A-5I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) yields eigenvectors proportional to \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) For \(\lambda=1\text{,}\) solving \((A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) yields eigenvectors proportional to \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    11.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ -3 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\) This matrix has complex eigenvalues. In this exercise, we show how to construct a real matrix \(P\) such that \(A\\) is similar over the reals to a matrix of the form \(C = \begin{bmatrix} a \amp -b \\ b \amp a \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Show that the eigenvalues of \(A\) are complex.
      🔗
    2. Choose one eigenvalue \(\lambda = a + bi\) and write down the corresponding matrix \(C\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Using the same eigenvalue, find a complex eigenvector \(\mathbf v\) by solving \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    4. Write \(\mathbf v = \mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\) have real entries.
      🔗
    5. Let \(P = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]\text{.}\) Verify directly that \(A\mathbf v_1 = a\mathbf v_1 - b\mathbf v_2\) and \(A\mathbf v_2 = b\mathbf v_1 + a\mathbf v_2\text{,}\) and conclude that \(A = PCP^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Hint.
    For part (a), compute \(\det(A - \lambda I)\text{.}\) For the final part, do not compute \(P^{-1}\) directly; instead, use how \(A\) acts on \(\mathbf v_1\) and \(\mathbf v_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are \(1 \pm i\sqrt{6}\text{.}\) One valid choice of \(P\) satisfies \(A = PCP^{-1}\) with
    \begin{equation*} C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -\sqrt{6} \\ \sqrt{6} \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We compute
      \begin{align*} \det(A - \lambda I) \amp = \det \begin{bmatrix} 1-\lambda \amp 2 \\ -3 \amp 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix} \\ \amp = (1-\lambda)^2 + 6. \end{align*}
      Setting this equal to zero gives \(\lambda = 1 \pm i\sqrt{6}\text{,}\) so the eigenvalues are complex.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Choosing \(\lambda = 1 + i\sqrt{6}\text{,}\) we have \(a = 1\) and \(b = \sqrt{6}\text{,}\) and therefore
      \begin{equation*} C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -\sqrt{6} \\ \sqrt{6} \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Solving \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\) with \(\lambda = 1 + i\sqrt{6}\) yields the eigenvector
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf v = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ i\sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Writing \(\mathbf v = \mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2\text{,}\) we obtain
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Let
      \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      We compute
      \begin{align*} A\mathbf v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} \\ \amp = \mathbf v_1 - \sqrt{6}\mathbf v_2, \end{align*}
      and
      \begin{align*} A\mathbf v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2\sqrt{6} \\ \sqrt{6} \end{bmatrix} \\ \amp = \sqrt{6}\mathbf v_1 + \mathbf v_2. \end{align*}
      These identities show that \(A[\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2] = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]C\text{,}\) and since \(P\) is invertible, we conclude that \(A = PCP^{-1}\text{.}\)
