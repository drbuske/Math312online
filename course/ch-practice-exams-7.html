    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Let \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) and determine the angle between the vectors.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute the dot product first. Then use \(\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -8\text{,}\) and \(\theta \approx 111.3^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Compute the dot product: \((2)(1) + (-1)(4) + (3)(-2) = 2 - 4 - 6 = -8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute magnitudes: \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{14}\text{,}\) \(\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{21}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then \(\cos\theta = \frac{-8}{\sqrt{14}\sqrt{21}}\text{,}\) so \(\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{-8}{\sqrt{294}}\right) \approx 111.3^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    Find a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and verify that its dot product with itself is 1.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Divide the vector by its magnitude.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The unit vector is \(\begin{bmatrix} -\frac{3}{5} \\ \frac{4}{5} \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and its dot product with itself is 1.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 4^2} = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The unit vector is \(\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{3}{5} \\ \frac{4}{5} \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Check: \(\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25} = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Determine whether \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) are orthogonal. If they are not, find the projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\text{.}\) If it is zero, the vectors are orthogonal. Otherwise use the projection formula.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vectors are orthogonal, so the projection is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Compute the dot product: \((1)(2) + (2)(-1) + (1)(0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the dot product is zero, the vectors are orthogonal.
    🔗
    Therefore, the projection is \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    4.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find a vector in the orthogonal complement of the row space of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    A vector in the orthogonal complement must be orthogonal to each row of \(A\text{.}\) Use dot products to form a system of equations.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    One such vector is \(\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Being orthogonal to the rows gives
    🔗
    \(\begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0 \\ x_2 + 3x_3 = 0 \end{aligned} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    From the second equation, \(x_2 = -3x_3\text{.}\) Substituting, \(x_1 - 7x_3 = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_1 = 7x_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus \(\mathbf{x} = x_3 \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) so choosing \(x_3 = 1\) gives the result.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Compute \(A^T\) and verify that \((A\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} \cdot (A^T\mathbf{y})\) for \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute both sides separately: find \(A\mathbf{x}\) and dot with \(\mathbf{y}\text{,}\) then compute \(A^T\mathbf{y}\) and dot with \(\mathbf{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and both sides evaluate to \(-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute the transpose: \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \\ -1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then \((A\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{y} = (1)(2) + (1)(0) + (-4)(1) = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now compute \(A^T \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then \(\mathbf{x} \cdot (A^T \mathbf{y}) = (1)(1) + (-1)(3) = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Both sides agree, verifying the identity.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    6.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 4 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    (a) Find a basis for the column space of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) What is the rank of \(A\text{?}\)
    🔗
    (c) Find a basis for the orthogonal complement of the column space of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Row reduce \(A\) to identify pivot columns for the column space. The orthogonal complement of the column space consists of all vectors \(\mathbf{x}\) satisfying \(A^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    A basis for the column space is \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    The rank of \(A\) is 2.
    🔗
    A basis for the orthogonal complement is \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The columns of \(A\) are \(\mathbf{c}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Observe that \(\mathbf{c}_3 = \mathbf{c}_1 + \mathbf{c}_2\) and \(\mathbf{c}_4 = 2\mathbf{c}_1 - \mathbf{c}_2\text{.}\) Therefore, the column space is spanned by \(\mathbf{c}_1\) and \(\mathbf{c}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hence a basis for the column space is \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    Since there are two pivot columns, the rank of \(A\) is 2.
    🔗
    To find the orthogonal complement, we solve \(A^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\text{.}\) This is equivalent to requiring \(\mathbf{x}\) to be orthogonal to \(\mathbf{c}_1\) and \(\mathbf{c}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \(\begin{aligned} x_1 + x_3 = 0 \\ 2x_1 + x_2 + 3x_3 = 0 \end{aligned} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    From the first equation, \(x_1 = -x_3\text{.}\) Substituting into the second, \(-2x_3 + x_2 + 3x_3 = x_2 + x_3 = 0\text{,}\) so \(x_2 = -x_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\mathbf{x} = x_3 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    A basis for the orthogonal complement is \(\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    7.
    Let \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{u}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\) Show that this is an orthogonal basis for \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{,}\) and write \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) as a linear combination of these vectors.
    🔗
    Hint.
    First verify that all pairwise dot products are zero. Then use \(c_i = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_i}{\mathbf{u}_i \cdot \mathbf{u}_i}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The vectors are orthogonal, and \(\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{u}_1 + 1\mathbf{u}_2 + 2\mathbf{u}_3.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Compute dot products: \(\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3 = 0\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3 = 0\text{.}\) So the vectors are orthogonal.
    🔗
    Now compute coefficients:
    🔗
    \(c_1 = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1} = \frac{3 + 1}{2} = 2,\)
    🔗
    \(c_2 = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2}{\mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2} = \frac{3 - 1}{2} = 1,\)
    🔗
    \(c_3 = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_3}{\mathbf{u}_3 \cdot \mathbf{u}_3} = 2.\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2 + 2\mathbf{u}_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    8.
    Let \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\) Suppose that these vectors form an orthogonal basis for a subspace \(W\) of \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Verify that \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{u}_2\) are orthogonal.
    🔗
    (b) Find the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) onto \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Write \(\mathbf{v}\) as the sum of a vector in \(W\) and a vector orthogonal to \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the orthogonal projection formula: \(\mathrm{proj}_W \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1}{\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{u}_1 + \frac{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}_2}{\mathbf{u}_2\cdot\mathbf{u}_2}\mathbf{u}_2\text{.}\) Then subtract from \(\mathbf{v}\) to find the orthogonal component.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) The vectors are orthogonal.
    🔗
    (b) \(\mathrm{proj}_W \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    (c) \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Compute \(\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = (1)(1) + (1)(-1) + (0)(0) = 0,\) so the vectors are orthogonal.
    🔗
    (b) Compute \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 3 + 1 + 0 = 4, \quad \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 2,\) and \(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 3 - 1 + 0 = 2, \quad \mathbf{u}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_2 = 2.\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\mathrm{proj}_W \mathbf{v} = \frac{4}{2}\mathbf{u}_1 + \frac{2}{2}\mathbf{u}_2 = 2\mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_2.\)
    🔗
    Compute: \(2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    (c) Then \(\mathbf{v} - \mathrm{proj}_W \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This vector is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{u}_2\text{,}\) so it lies in \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    9.
    Apply the Gram–Schmidt process to the vectors
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    to produce an orthogonal basis for the subspace they span.
    🔗
    Then normalize your result to produce an orthonormal basis.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Set \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\text{.}\) Then compute \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{v}_2\) using \(\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{u}_1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    An orthogonal basis is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \\ -\tfrac{1}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    An orthonormal basis is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\ -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\ \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{6}} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Start with \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1, \quad \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1} \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \\ \tfrac{1}{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1} \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \\ \tfrac{1}{2} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \\ -\tfrac{1}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This gives an orthogonal basis \(\{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now normalize:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|\mathbf{u}_1\| = \sqrt{2}, \quad \mathbf{e}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Also \(\|\mathbf{u}_2\| = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} + 1} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}.\)
    🔗
    Thus \(\mathbf{e}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3/2}} \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \\ -\tfrac{1}{2} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\ -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\ \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{6}} \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hence \(\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2\}\) is an orthonormal basis.
    🔗

    10.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\) Compute the QR factorization of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    That is, find an orthonormal matrix \(Q\) and an upper triangular matrix \(R\) such that \(A = QR\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Apply Gram–Schmidt to the columns of \(A\text{.}\) You should find that the projection is especially simple, and the coefficients in \(R\) come out as integers.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(Q = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad R = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} \amp \sqrt{2} \\ 0 \amp \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let the columns of \(A\) be \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Set \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\text{.}\) Then \(\|\mathbf{u}_1\| = \sqrt{2}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{e}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Compute \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{v}_2.\)
    🔗
    \(\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 2, \quad \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1 = 2,\) so \(\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Thus \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Compute \(\|\mathbf{u}_2\| = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{3},\) so \(\mathbf{e}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Thus \(Q = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \amp -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ 0 \amp \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Now compute \(R = Q^T A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(r_{11} = \|\mathbf{v}_1\| = \sqrt{2}, \quad r_{12} = \mathbf{e}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = \sqrt{2},\) and \(r_{22} = \|\mathbf{u}_2\| = \sqrt{3}.\)
    🔗
    Therefore \(R = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{2} \amp \sqrt{2} \\ 0 \amp \sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hence, \(A = QR\text{.}\)

    11.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Find the least squares solution to the system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then compute the vector \(A\mathbf{x}\) and interpret your result.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute \(A^T A\) and \(A^T \mathbf{b}\text{,}\) then solve the normal equations \(A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The least squares solution is \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Then \(A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Next compute \(A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot1 + 0\cdot2 \\ 1\cdot2 + 0\cdot1 + 1\cdot2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Solve the normal equations: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    From the first equation: \(2x_1 + x_2 = 3\text{.}\) From the second: \(x_1 + 2x_2 = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solving gives \(x_1 = 1\text{,}\) \(x_2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Compute \(A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This vector is the projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto the column space of \(A\text{.}\) It is the closest vector in the column space to \(\mathbf{b}\text{.}\)

    12.
    A company tracks the number of hours an employee trains and the number of tasks completed during a shift. The data collected are:
    🔗
    \((1,2), (2,2), (3,4), (4,5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Fit a line \(y = c + mt\) to this data using the method of least squares.
    🔗
    Interpret the meaning of the slope and intercept in this context.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Form a matrix equation \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) where \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp t_1 \\ 1 \amp t_2 \\ 1 \amp t_3 \\ 1 \amp t_4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Then compute \(A^T A\) and \(A^T \mathbf{b}\text{,}\) and solve \(A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The least squares line is \(y = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{11}{10}t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The slope indicates that each additional hour of training increases output by about 1.1 tasks, and the intercept suggests about one-half of a task is completed with no training (a baseline level).
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We model the relationship as \(y = c + mt\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \\ 1 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    \(A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 10 \\ 10 \amp 30 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 38 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Solve \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 10 \\ 10 \amp 30 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c \\ m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 13 \\ 38 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Multiply the first equation by 5: \(20c + 50m = 65\text{,}\) and the second by 2: \(20c + 60m = 76\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Subtracting gives \(10m = 11\text{,}\) so \(m = \frac{11}{10}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Substitute back: \(4c + 10\cdot\frac{11}{10} = 13\text{,}\) so \(4c = 2\text{,}\) and \(c = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, the least squares line is \(y = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{11}{10}t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This line gives the best linear approximation to the data, minimizing the total squared error between observed and predicted values.
