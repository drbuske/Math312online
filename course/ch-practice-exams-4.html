    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Consider the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 1 \\ 5 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Determine whether \(A\) is invertible. If it is, find \(A^{-1}\) using row reduction.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Form the augmented matrix \([A \mid I]\) and row reduce. If the left side reduces to the identity matrix, then \(A\) is invertible.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The matrix \(A\) is invertible, and \(A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -1 \\ -5 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin with the augmented matrix \(\left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 2 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 5 \amp 3 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{array} \right].\)
    🔗
    After performing row operations to reduce the left side to the identity, we obtain \(\left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 \amp 0 \amp 3 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -5 \amp 2 \end{array} \right].\)
    🔗
    Since the left side is the identity matrix, \(A\) is invertible, and the right side is \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \\ 2 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Show that \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
    2. Solve the equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) using \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    First find \(A^{-1}\text{.}\) Then multiply both sides of \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) on the left by \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The matrix \(A\) is invertible, and the solution is \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The inverse of \(A\) is \(A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    We compute \(\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Suppose \(A\) is a square matrix and there exists a vector \(\mathbf{b}\) such that the equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) has more than one solution.
    🔗
    Explain why \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Consider what invertibility would imply about the number of solutions to \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    If \(A\) were invertible, then multiplying both sides of \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) by \(A^{-1}\) would yield \(\mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This equation would produce a unique solution. Since the system has more than one solution, this contradicts the existence of an inverse.
    🔗
    Therefore, \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    🔗

    4.
    Let \(\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} \right\}\) be a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Find the coordinate vector of \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix}\) relative to the basis \(\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Write \(\mathbf{v}\) as a linear combination of the basis vectors and solve for the coefficients.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \([\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We seek scalars \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) such that \(c_1 \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} + c_2 \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    This gives the system \(\begin{cases} c_1 + 2c_2 = 3 \\ c_1 - c_2 = 0 \end{cases}.\)
    🔗
    Solving, we find \(c_1=1\) and \(c_2=1\text{.}\) Therefore, \([\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    Let \(\mathcal{B}\) be a basis of a vector space \(V\text{.}\) Suppose that a vector \(\mathbf{v}\in V\) satisfies \([\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\-1\\2\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Explain why \(\mathbf{v}\) cannot have a different coordinate vector relative to \(\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Recall the definition of a basis and what it implies about linear combinations.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The coordinate vector of \(\mathbf{v}\) relative to \(\mathcal{B}\) is unique.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\mathcal{B}\) is a basis, it is linearly independent. This means that there is exactly one way to express \(\mathbf{v}\) as a linear combination of the vectors in \(\mathcal{B}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Coordinates record the coefficients in this linear combination. If there were two different coordinate vectors for \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) then \(\mathbf{v}\) would have two distinct representations as a linear combination of the basis vectors.
    🔗
    This would contradict linear independence. Therefore, the coordinate vector is unique.
    🔗

    6.
    Let \(\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} \right\}\) be a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The coordinate vector of \(\mathbf{v}\) relative to \(\mathcal{B}\) is \(\mathbf{v}_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Find \(\mathbf{v}\) in standard coordinates.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the definition of coordinate vectors to reconstruct the actual vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition, \(\mathbf{v} = 2 \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} - 1 \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    7.
    In the Figure 47, the vectors \(\mathbf{b}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix} \text{and } \mathbf{b}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\) are shown as arrows in the plane. These vectors form a basis \(\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2\}\) for \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Construct the change‑of‑basis matrix \(P_{\mathcal{B}}\) that converts \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates into standard coordinates.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use this matrix to find the standard coordinates of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) whose \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinates are \([\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    The columns of the change‑of‑basis matrix are the basis vectors written in standard coordinates. Then multiply the matrix by the coordinate vector.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(P_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\2\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The change‑of‑basis matrix from \(\mathcal{B}\) to the standard basis is formed by placing the basis vectors as columns: \(P_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. To recover the vector in standard coordinates, we compute \(\mathbf{v} = P_{\mathcal{B}}[\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.\)
      🔗
      Carrying out the multiplication gives \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 + 1 \\ 3 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
      🔗
      This matches what we would expect geometrically from the diagram: the vector is three copies of \(\mathbf{b}_1\) minus one copy of \(\mathbf{b}_2\text{.}\)

    8.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Compute \(\det(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Interpret the value of the determinant as the area of a parallelogram and describe its orientation.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a \amp b\\ c \amp d\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) the determinant is \(ad - bc\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\det(A)=4\text{.}\) The parallelogram has area 4 and is positively oriented.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute \(\det(A) = (3)(2) - (1)(2) = 6 - 2 = 4.\)
    🔗
    The determinant gives the signed area of the parallelogram formed by the columns of \(A\text{.}\) Since the value is positive, the orientation is positive, and the area of the parallelogram is 4.
    🔗

    9.
    Consider the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Compute \(\det(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Explain what your answer tells you about whether \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    A square matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\det(A)=0\text{,}\) so \(A\) is not invertible.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute \(\det(A) = (2)(2) - (4)(1) = 4 - 4 = 0.\)
    🔗
    Since the determinant is zero, the area of the parallelogram formed by the columns of \(A\) is zero. This means the columns are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    Therefore, \(A\) is not invertible.

    10.
    A matrix \(A\) represents a linear transformation of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) Suppose \(\det(A) = -3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Describe how this transformation affects:
    1. area
      🔗
      🔗
    2. orientation
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    The absolute value of the determinant gives the area scaling factor, and the sign gives the orientation.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Areas are scaled by a factor of 3 and orientation is reversed.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The determinant describes how a linear transformation affects area and orientation.
    🔗
    Since \(|\det(A)| = 3\text{,}\) areas are scaled by a factor of 3. Any region in the plane will have three times the area after the transformation.
    🔗
    Because the determinant is negative, the transformation reverses orientation. This corresponds geometrically to a reflection combined with a scaling.
    🔗
    Consider, for example, the linear transformation \(T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    We can use this matrix to illustrate how a determinant of \(-3\) affects area and orientation in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First, we compute the determinant of \(A\text{:}\) \(\det(A) = (0)(0) - (3)(1) = -3.\) Because the off‑diagonal entry involves a sign change due to the column order, the transformation reverses orientation, giving an effective determinant of \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To see the geometric effect, consider how \(A\) acts on the standard basis vectors: \(A \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    The unit square determined by the standard basis vectors is mapped to a parallelogram with base length 3 and height 1. Therefore, the area is multiplied by a factor of 3.
    🔗
    Since the image of the first basis vector rotates counterclockwise while the second flips direction across the axis, the orientation of the plane is reversed. This corresponds to the negative sign of the determinant.
    🔗
    Thus, the transformation represented by \(A\) expands area by a factor of 3 and reverses orientation, consistent with a determinant of \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    11.
    Compute the determinant of the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 0 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 3 \amp 0 \\ 1 \amp 4 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    You should use cofactor expansion to simplify your work.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Choose a row or column with the most zeros before expanding.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\det(A) = 15.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We expand along the second row, since it contains two zeros. The only nonzero entry is \(a_{22}=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By cofactor expansion, \(\det(A) = 3 \cdot (-1)^{2+2} \det \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    The remaining determinant is \((2)(2) - (-1)(1) = 4 + 1 = 5.\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\det(A) = 3 \cdot 5 = 15.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    12.
    Compute the determinant of the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp 6 \\ 7 \amp 8 \amp 10 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Use cofactor expansion to evaluate the determinant.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Choose any row or column for cofactor expansion. Expanding along the first row keeps the arithmetic manageable.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\det(A) = -3.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We expand the determinant along the first row: \(\det(A) = 1\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp 6 \\ 8 \amp 10 \end{bmatrix} - 2\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 6 \\ 7 \amp 10 \end{bmatrix} + 3\cdot \det \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 5 \\ 7 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    We now compute each \(2\times2\) determinant: \(\det \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp 6 \\ 8 \amp 10 \end{bmatrix} = 50 - 48 = 2,\) \(\det \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 6 \\ 7 \amp 10 \end{bmatrix} = 40 - 42 = -2,\) \(\det \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp 5 \\ 7 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix} = 32 - 35 = -3.\)
    🔗
    Substituting back, \(\det(A) = 1(2) - 2(-2) + 3(-3) = 2 + 4 - 9 = -3.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    13.
    Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    1. Find a basis for the column space of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is the dimension of the column space?
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    Row‑reduce the matrix to identify which columns contain pivots. The corresponding original columns form a basis for the column space.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    A basis for \(\operatorname{Col}(A)\) is given by the first two columns of \(A\text{.}\) The dimension is 2.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We row‑reduce \(A\text{:}\) \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -7 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 3 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    The pivot columns are columns 1 and 2. Therefore, the corresponding original columns form a basis for the column space: \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    Since there are two basis vectors, the dimension of \(\operatorname{Col}(A)\) is 2.
    🔗
    🔗

    14.
    Find a basis for the null space of the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp -2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Solve the homogeneous system \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) and express the solution in parametric vector form.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    A basis for the null space is \(\left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\2\end{bmatrix} \right\}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We row‑reduce the augmented matrix: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 1 \\ 4 \amp -2 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    The equation is \(2x_1 - x_2 + x_3 = 0.\) Solving for \(x_1\) gives \(x_1=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_2 - x_3).\)
    🔗
    Letting \(x_2=s\) and \(x_3=t\text{,}\) the solution is \(\mathbf{x} = s\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\2\end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    These vectors form a basis for the null space.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    15.
    Let \(A\) be a \(3\times4\) matrix whose column space has dimension 2.
    🔗
    1. How many free variables does the homogeneous system \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\) have?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. What is the dimension of the null space of \(A\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint.
    Use the relationship between pivot columns, free variables, and dimensions.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    There are 2 free variables, and the null space has dimension 2.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The dimension of the column space equals the number of pivot columns. Since the column space has dimension 2, there are 2 pivot columns.
    🔗
    Because \(A\) has 4 columns total, the number of free variables is \(4 - 2 = 2.\)
    🔗
    The dimension of the null space equals the number of free variables. Therefore, the null space has dimension 2.
    🔗
    This relationship reflects the fundamental balance between the column space and null space emphasized in this section.
