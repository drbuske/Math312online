    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Consider the system of equations:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 3x_3 \mathstrut \amp = -2 \\ -3x_1 + x_2 + 2x_3 \mathstrut \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    1. Write this system as a matrix equation \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Using row reduction, find a parametric description of the intersection of these two planes.
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -2 \amp 3 \\ -3 \amp 1 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Put the following augmented matrix in RREF:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -2 \amp 3 \amp -2 \\ -3 \amp 1 \amp 2 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp -7/3 \amp 2/3 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -5 \amp 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      The solution to this system can be described parametrically by
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + x_3 \begin{bmatrix} 7/3 \\ 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    Determine weights to make a true statement below, or explain why this cannot be done.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \underline{\hspace{8mm}}\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\4\end{bmatrix} + \underline{\hspace{8mm}}\begin{bmatrix}7\\-36\\29\end{bmatrix} + \underline{\hspace{8mm}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\6\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}10\\21\\8\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Form an augmented matrix and row reduce:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 7 \amp 1 \amp 10 \\ -1 \amp -36 \amp 6 \amp 21 \\ 4 \amp 29 \amp -1 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1.2 \amp 7.8 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -0.2 \amp -0.8 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    So we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 7.8 \begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\\4\end{bmatrix} + -0.8 \begin{bmatrix}7\\-36\\29\end{bmatrix} + 0 \begin{bmatrix}1\\6\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}10\\21\\8\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    by choosing the free variable \(x_3 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Suppose that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \amp 4 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 6 \amp 8 \\ 1 \amp 3 \amp 4 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    1. Compute \(rank(A)\text{.}\) Explain how you calculated this.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(Nul(A)\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^k\) for \(k =\underline{\hspace{8mm}}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Compute the dimension of \(Nul(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find a basis for \(Col(A)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Find a basis for \(Col(A)^\perp\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. \begin{equation*} A \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 1 \amp 2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      so that rank(\(A\)) = 2 since there are 2 rows containing a pivot.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(A\) has 4 columns so that \(Nul(A)\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^4\text{.}\) That is \(k = 4\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The dimension of \(Nul(A)\) = 4 - rank(\(A\)) = 4 - 2 = 2.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The pivot columns from the original matrix \(A\) form a basis for \(Col(A)\text{.}\) That is the basis is given by
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\3\end{bmatrix} \right\} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    5. \(Col(A)^\perp\) is the null space of \(A^T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Since \(A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp 3 \\ 3 \amp 6 \amp 4 \\ 4 \amp 8 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}\) we can solve \(A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}\) for \(\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\) But since
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} A^T \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      we have one free variable and a basis for the set of solutions, which is precisely a basis for \(Col(A)^\perp\text{,}\) is given by
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix} \right\} \end{equation*}
      Is it easy to check that this vector is both perpendicular to each vector found in the basis for \(Col(A)\) and linearly independent if placed in a set with those vectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    4.
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 2a - 4b - 3c \\ 4a - 6b \\ 9a - b + 7c \end{bmatrix} : a,b,c \text{ are real} \right\} \end{equation*}
    Explain why \(H\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} H = span \left\{ \begin{bmatrix}1\\4\\9\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}-4\\-6\\-1\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}-3\\0\\7\end{bmatrix} \right\} \end{equation*}
    so it is a subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    Define a specific linear transformation \(T\) and use a determinant to find the area of the parallelogram with vertices \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((4,1)\text{,}\) \((2,4)\text{,}\) and \((-2,3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Define a linear transformation \(T\) so that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{e_1}) = \begin{bmatrix}4\\1\end{bmatrix}, \quad T(\mathbf{e_2}) = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\3\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -2 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    The area of the parallelogram is then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A) = 12 - (-2) = 14 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    6.
    Let \(\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \mathbf{v_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\, \mathbf{v_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Is \(\mathcal{B}\) a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{?}\) Defend your answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find a \(2 \times 2\) matrix that will convert \(B\)-coordinate vectors to standard coordinate vectors. That is, find a matrix \(P\) with \(\mathbf{y}=P\left\{ \mathbf{y}\right\}_{\mathcal{B}}\) for every vector \(\mathbf{y}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find a \(2 \times 2\) matrix that will convert standard coordinate vectors to \(\mathcal{B}\)-coordinate vectors. That is, find a matrix \(Q\) with \(\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{B}}=Q\left\{ \mathbf{y}\right\}\) for every vector \(\mathbf{y}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(\{y\}_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) find \(y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. If \(y = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) find \(\{y\}_{\mathcal{B}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. Yes, the vectors are linearly independent and span \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) If they were the columns in a matrix \(A\text{,}\) the matrix in the RREF would be the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix with a pivot in each row and in each column.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle Q = P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \amp \tfrac{1}{4} \\ \tfrac{1}{2} \amp -\tfrac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 1 \\ 2 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -10 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \{\mathbf{y}\}_{\mathcal{B}} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \amp \tfrac{1}{4} \\ \tfrac{1}{2} \amp -\tfrac{1}{4} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.25 \\ 0.75 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    7.
    The elementary matrices \(E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ -2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(E_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -\tfrac{4}{3} \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\) transform the matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 6 \\ 4 \amp 5 \amp 6 \\ 0 \amp -4 \amp -8 \end{bmatrix}\) to \(U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 6 \\ 0 \amp -3 \amp -6 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) That is, \(E_2 E_1 A = U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find the LU-factorization of \(A\text{.}\) That is, determine a lower triangular matrix \(L\) so that \(A=LU\) with \(A\) and \(U\) as given above.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Is \(A\) invertible? How can you decide without attempting to find the inverse of \(A\) through computation?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Use the LU-factorization to solve \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) for \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ 19 \\ -12 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle L = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \tfrac{4}{3} \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \tfrac{4}{3} \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. No. \(\det(U) = 0\) since it has a 0 row and the product of its diagonal entries is 0. So det(A) is also 0 and hence not invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We solve \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) as \(LU\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\text{.}\) Set \(\mathbf{y}=U\mathbf{x}\text{.}\) Then \(L\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b}\text{.}\) That is, we solve the following via forward substitution:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ 19 \\ -12 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      This gives \(\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ -9 \\ -21 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) We then use back substitution to find \(\mathbf{x}\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp 4 \amp 6 \\ 0 \amp -3 \amp -6 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ -9 \\ -21 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      This gives \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 11.5 \\ -18 \\ 10.5 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    8.
    A \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_1=-2\) with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\lambda_2=3\) with eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find \(A^4 \begin{bmatrix}5\\-1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Hint: Do not try to find \(A\text{.}\) The point is to do this without actually computing \(A^4\text{.}\) Instead, write \(\begin{bmatrix}5\\-1\end{bmatrix}\) as a linear combination of \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To write \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}\) as a linear combination of \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\text{,}\) we use an augmented matrix:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 1 \amp 5 \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 6 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -13 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So,
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = 6 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - 13 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    which means that
    \begin{align*} A^4 \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \mathstrut \amp = A^4 \left( 6 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - 13 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\ \mathstrut \amp = 6(-2)^4 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} -13(3)^4 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ \mathstrut \amp = 96 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} -1053 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ \mathstrut \amp = \begin{bmatrix} -765 \\ -861 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    9.
    Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2 \amp -2 \\ -2 \amp 5\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Find the eigenvalues of \(A\) and find a basis for each eigenspace. Then, identify a diagonal matrix \(D\) and an orthogonal matrix \(P\) such that \(A=PDP^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-1)\) so that the eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(\lambda_1=6\) and \(\lambda_2=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A basis for the eigenspace \(E_{\{6\}}\) is \(\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\right\}\) and a basis for the eigenspace \(E_{\{1\}}\) is \(\left\{\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So we can diagonalize \(A\) using \(D=\begin{bmatrix}6 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and \(P=\begin{bmatrix}1 \amp 2 \\ -2 \amp 1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    10.
    Consider the matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}6 \amp 8 \\ -1 \amp 2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find the characteristic polynomial of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find a (complex) eigenvector for \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find an invertible matrix \(P\) and a matrix \(C=\begin{bmatrix}a \amp -b \\ b \amp a\end{bmatrix}\) such that \(A=PCP^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(\displaystyle \det(A- \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 6 - \lambda \amp 8 \\ -1 \amp 2 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 8\lambda + 20\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Using the quadratic formula to solve \(\lambda^2 -8\lambda+20 =\) gives \(\lambda = 4 \pm 2i\) (two complex eigenvalues).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For \(\lambda = 4 + 2i\text{,}\) we solve \((A- \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\) using an augmented matrix:
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2i \amp 8 \amp 0 \\ -1 \amp -2 - 2i \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 + 2i \amp 0 \\ 2 - 2i \amp 8 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 + 2i \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      so that an eigenvector of \(A\) is
      \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 2i \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - i \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Here \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -2 \\ -1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\) so that \(P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -1 \\ -\tfrac{1}{2} \amp -1 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(C = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -2 \\ 2 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    11.
    For the following, decide if the statement is True or False. If it is false, give a counterexample. If it is true, explain why.
    🔗
    1. The sum of two eigenvectors of a matrix \(A\) is always an eigenvector of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The sum of two eigenvectors of a matrix \(A\) is never an eigenvector of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. False. In general, if two eigenvectors correspond to the same eigenvalue, any linear combination (including their sum) is an eigenvector.

      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the matrix \(P\) that projects vectors orthogonally onto \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find the closest point to \(y = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 10 \\ 18 \end{bmatrix}\) in the subspace \(U\) of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) spanned by the orthogonal basis vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Find the orthogonal projection of \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) onto \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    1.     16.
    Apply the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process to the following basis for a 3-dimensional subspace \(W\) of \(\mathbb{R}^4\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left\{ \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Start by setting \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\text{.}\) Then, define
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{u}_2 \mathstrut \amp = \mathbf{v}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{v}_2 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1}{\mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{u}_1} \mathbf{u}_1 \\ \mathstrut \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{6}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ \mathstrut \amp = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    🔗
